## Artefact 1: The Four Pillars of Intellectual Property

🎥 **Watch the video:** *The four pillars of intellectual property: patents, trademarks, copyright, trade secrets*




**Summary**
This video explains the four main categories of IP protection: patents (novel inventions, 20 years), trademarks (brand identifiers, renewable 10-year terms), copyright (creative works, life plus 70 years) and trade secrets (confidential know-how, no time limit). It frames each as a different trade-off between rewarding an individual creator and eventually returning the work to public use.

**Reflection and justification**
This connects directly to the opening slide of our workshop, where we discussed how giving creators time-limited rights stimulates creativity, but society benefits most once inventions enter the public domain (Venugopal 2026). Before the workshop I assumed IP protection existed purely to reward creators. Discussing the trade-off in slide 3 shifted that: protection is really a compromise between individual incentive and public benefit, not an unconditional reward.

I chose this video because, as a future ICT professional, I will need to judge which protection fits a product: patenting an algorithm (public, time-limited) versus guarding it as a trade secret, like Coca-Cola's formula discussed in our workshop (Venugopal 2026). Practically, this means that when I build software in my career, choosing between open-sourcing an algorithm and treating it as confidential IP is an ethical decision about public benefit versus commercial advantage, not just a technical or legal one.

## Artefact 2: Authors Have Mixed Feelings About the $1.5B Anthropic Copyright Ruling

📰 **Read the article:** Veltman (2026)




**Summary**
Veltman (2026) reports that AI company Anthropic agreed to pay US$1.5 billion to settle a class action from over 300,000 authors after a court found it had trained its Claude models on pirated books. The judge ruled that training on legally acquired books is fair use, but using pirated copies is not, meaning the ruling was only a partial win for authors and left the harder question of AI competing with human writers unresolved.

**Reflection and justification**
I chose this to apply the Rule Utilitarian Analysis from our workshop (Venugopal 2026, slide 42) to a live case. The workshop's logic was: if software is copied freely, developers earn less, less new software gets made, and society is harmed. The same reasoning applies here: if training data can be used without compensating authors, fewer new books get written, which harms society by reducing the works available to it.

Ethically, this case is less about whether the copying was legal and more about fairness to the people whose work made the model's ability to write possible. This is a good example of the point our workshop closed on: ethics and the law are not the same and do not always align (Venugopal 2026). The court decided a narrow legal question (was the *acquisition* of the books lawful), but it did not resolve the ethical question of whether creators are fairly compensated when their work trains a commercial system. As a future ICT professional, this taught me that "is it legal" and "is it fair to the people whose work made this possible" can have very different answers, and that I need to ask both questions, not just the first one, when working with any system trained on other people's data.

## Artefact 3: The Algorithmic Muse and the Public Domain

📄 **Read the journal article:** Elmahjub (2025)

*[Insert screenshot of the article here]*

**Summary**
Elmahjub (2025, p. 2) argues that courts consistently reject non-human authorship, pointing to *Naruto v Slater* — the same monkey-selfie case discussed in our workshop — as precedent for why an AI cannot hold copyright either. Because generative AI produces outputs through probabilistic prediction rather than direct human expression, the article concludes raw AI output should default to the public domain, with copyright attaching only to a human's own creative edits (Elmahjub 2025, p. 8).

**Reflection and justification**
This article showed me that the monkey selfie case is not a quirky one-off; it set the exact legal principle — no legal person, no copyright — that courts now reuse for AI (Venugopal 2026). It sharpened the position I argued in the workshop discussion: ownership should depend on how much creative decision-making was genuinely the human's, not just who triggered the output.

This matters ethically for accountability too. If AI is misused to create harmful content about a real person, responsibility should sit with the human who directed it, not the tool, since the tool cannot be held legally or morally responsible (Venugopal 2026). For my future ICT career, this shapes how I would attribute and license AI-assisted work responsibly: crediting the human decisions behind an AI-assisted output, and being clear-eyed that "the AI made it" is not a defence against accountability for how it was used.

*The famous "monkey selfie" at the centre of Naruto v Slater, discussed in our Week 7 workshop*

**Summary**
Our workshop traced the history of copyright creep in the United States, then examined the monkey selfie dispute: photographer David Slater set up the camera, but a macaque pressed the shutter, and courts ruled animals cannot hold copyright since the law is written for humans (Venugopal 2026). The tutor then extended this to generative AI: if a non-human cannot own copyright, do AI-created images belong to no one, to the prompter, or to the AI developer?

**Reflection and justification**
Before this workshop, I had never questioned the assumption that whoever "creates" something automatically owns it. I assumed authorship was about who physically produced the output: the photographer who owned the camera, or in the AI case, whoever typed the prompt.

The monkey selfie case changed that. The court did not ask who caused the photo to exist; it asked whether the creator was human at all, and ruled that copyright is a legal construct built around human authorship, not a neutral reward for effort or cause (Venugopal 2026). That reframing directly answered the question our tutor posed: if an animal cannot hold copyright because it isn't human, then an AI cannot hold copyright either, for the same reason (Elmahjub 2025, p. 2). What shifted my view further during the discussion was realising that ownership should scale with how much genuine creative decision-making the human contributed, rather than defaulting automatically to whoever triggered the output. For example, if someone uses AI as a mere tool while making the real creative decisions themselves, they deserve the resulting rights. But if someone directs an AI to produce something unethical, such as harmful imagery of a real person, the same logic means the human bears responsibility, not the AI, because it was the human's decision that caused the harm.

For my ICT career, this discussion changed how I will treat AI-assisted work going forward. I now think about this every time I use AI in an assignment, a personal project, or content I post online: how much of the final decision-making was actually mine, and would I be comfortable claiming ownership of it, or being accountable for it, on that basis? That is the everyday ethical awareness this unit is asking us to build, and it is now something I actively apply rather than something I take for granted.
