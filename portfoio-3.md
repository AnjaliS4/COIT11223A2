## Artefact 1: The Four Pillars of Intellectual Property

🎥 [Watch the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uvWvtoX-b24)

![The four pillars of intellectual property: patents, trademarks, copyright, trade secrets](images/artefact1-ip-pillars.png)

**Summary:**
This video explains the four main categories of IP protection: patents (novel inventions, 20 years), trademarks (brand identifiers, renewable 10-year terms), copyright (creative works, life plus 70 years) and trade secrets (confidential know-how, no time limit). It frames each as a different trade-off between rewarding an individual creator and eventually returning the work to public use.

**Reflection and justification:**
This connects to our workshop's opening slide on IP protection: society gives creators time-limited rights as an incentive but benefits most when inventions eventually enter the public domain. I chose this video because, as a future ICT professional, I will need to judge which protection fits a product — patenting an algorithm (public, time-limited) versus guarding it as a trade secret like Coca-Cola's formula from our workshop (private, indefinite). Ethically, this balances an individual's reward against the public's access to knowledge.

## Artefact 2: Authors Have Mixed Feelings About the $1.5B Anthropic Copyright Ruling

📰 [Read the article](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/27/nx-s1-5904606/anthropic-vs-bartz-ai-copyright-lawsuit-pros-cons)

![Anthropic copyright settlement: $1.5 billion total, 300,000+ authors covered, $3,100 paid per book title](images/artefact2-anthropic-settlement.png)

**Summary:**
Veltman (2026) reports that AI company Anthropic agreed to pay US$1.5 billion to settle a class action from over 300,000 authors after a court found it had trained its Claude models on pirated books. The judge ruled that training on legally acquired books is fair use, but using pirated copies is not, meaning the ruling was only a partial win for authors and left the harder question of AI competing with human writers unresolved.

**Reflection and justification:**
I chose this because it let me apply the Rule Utilitarian Analysis from our workshop (slide 42) to a live case: if training data can be copied freely, authors are paid less, so fewer new books get written, harming society — the same logic used there to argue that copying software is wrong. Ethically, this case is less about whether the copying was legal and more about fairness to the authors whose books gave the model its ability to write. It taught me that "is it legal" and "is it fair to the people whose work made this possible" can have very different answers.

## Artefact 3: The Algorithmic Muse and the Public Domain

📄 [Read the journal article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clsr.2025.106170)

![Screenshot from the workshop: an AI image generator refusing to create an image of a copyrighted character](images/artefact3-grogu-refusal.png)

**Summary:**
Elmahjub (2025, p. 2) argues that courts consistently reject non-human authorship, pointing to *Naruto v Slater* — the same monkey-selfie case from our workshop — as precedent for why an AI cannot hold copyright either. Because generative AI produces outputs through probabilistic prediction rather than direct human expression, the article concludes raw AI output should default to the public domain, with copyright attaching only to a human's own creative edits (Elmahjub 2025, p. 8).

**Reflection and justification:**
This article showed me the monkey selfie case is not a quirky one-off; it set the exact legal principle — no legal person, no copyright — that courts now reuse for AI. It sharpened my own workshop view that ownership should depend on how much creative decision-making was genuinely the human's, not just who pressed the button. This matters ethically for accountability too: if AI is misused to create harmful content about a real person, responsibility should sit with the human who directed it, not the tool. For my future ICT career, this shapes how I would attribute and license AI-assisted work responsibly.

## Artefact 4: Workshop Reflection – Who Owns an AI-Generated Image?

**Workshop:** Week 7 | Thursday, 3 September 2026 | Tutor: Umapathy Venugopal | Campus: Sydney

![Workshop Selfie](images/workshop-selfie.jpg)

![The famous "monkey selfie" at the centre of Naruto v Slater, discussed in our Week 7 workshop](images/artefact4-monkey-selfie.png)

**Summary:**
Our workshop traced the history of copyright creep in the US, then examined the monkey selfie dispute: photographer David Slater set up the camera, but a macaque pressed the shutter, and courts ruled animals cannot hold copyright since the law is written for humans. The tutor then extended this to generative AI: if a non-human can't own copyright, do AI-created images belong to no one, to the prompter, or to the AI developer?

**Reflection and justification:**
I chose this as my workshop artefact because it changed a belief I had never questioned: that whoever "creates" something automatically owns it. The monkey selfie case shows copyright is a human-centred legal construct, not a neutral reward for effort. My own position, argued in the discussion, is that ownership should scale with how much genuine creative decision-making the human contributed, rather than defaulting to whoever triggered the output — the standard Artefact 3 confirms courts now apply to AI. Since this workshop, I have caught myself applying this question to my own assignments and social media posts, which is exactly the everyday ethical awareness this unit is asking us to build.
