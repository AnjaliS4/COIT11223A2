## Introduction 
What is ADM? Automated decision-making occurs when a computer program makes an administrative decision, or part of one. It can make a decision on its own, suggest one, assist a human decision-maker or automate the fact-finding process. This includes everything from a basic rules-based calculator to AI and machine learning (Commonwealth Ombudsman 2025a, p. 7).

Why it is important for me as an ICT professional. Government ADM can decide things like income support, debt notices and fraud investigations. According to the ACS Code of Professional Conduct, the public interest comes before the interests of the business or the individual, and I should know the impact of my work on the public and consider their interests (Australian Computer Society 2014, pp. 5-6). The ethical theories from this unit give me a way to test that. Kantianism asks whether people are being used as a means to save money. Act Utilitarianism asks me to tally the harm to all affected parties. Social Contract Theory (Rawls) asks whether the least advantaged are being protected.

Before and after:
Prior to this unit I believed a computer decision would be more neutral and fair than a human decision, and that if a system followed its rules, the result would be acceptable. Now I see that a system can follow its rules perfectly and still break the law or hurt people, and that "it passed the technical requirements" is not the same as "it is ethical". This is the same lesson as in my Question 1 video.

### Summary of the artefact
This media release was published by the Commonwealth Ombudsman on 6 August 2025 about the Targeted Compliance Framework for job seekers. From April 2022 to July 2024, payments were cancelled automatically without the oversight the law required. The Ombudsman found these cancellations were not lawful. It made seven recommendations, which DEWR and Services Australia accepted (Commonwealth Ombudsman 2025b).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I selected this because it shows ADM harm still happening after Robodebt. The law had changed but the computer system had not, so the system broke the law. That links to clause 1.2.4(c) of the ACS Code, which requires me to know the relevant legislation and act in line with it (Australian Computer Society 2014, p. 7). It also fails the Kantian test, since vulnerable people were treated as a means to efficiency.

[Decision making better oractice guide](https://www.ombudsman.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0025/317437/Automated-Decision-Making-Better-Practice-Guide-March-2025.pdf)
### Summary of the artefact
The guide was updated in March 2025 by the Commonwealth Ombudsman, together with the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner and the Attorney-General's Department. It outlines how agencies should design, operate and monitor automated systems. It is based on the principles of legality, fairness, transparency, privacy and human oversight, and says that people should be able to challenge a decision made with automated assistance (Commonwealth Ombudsman 2025a, pp. 8-12, 46).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I picked this to see what good ADM in the Australian government is supposed to look like. When read alongside Artefact 1, there is a gap between guidance and practice. The checklist in the guide (pp. 48-59) is like a moral rule that Rule Utilitarianism would endorse, since if everyone followed it, harm would be reduced. It also matches my Question 1 answer, which was to test for bias before launch, not after.

[Carney (2026) on access to justice after the Robodebt Royal Commission](https://law.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0006/5630883/Carney-MULR-10.pdf)
### Summary of the artefact
In this journal article, Terry Carney tests whether the reforms that followed the Robodebt Royal Commission would protect people from another ADM failure. He finds that ways to resolve individual grievances have been restored, but systemic harm has been overlooked (Carney 2026, p. 14). He also warns that relying on the bureaucracy to fix itself shows 'heroic optimism' (Carney 2026, p. 40).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I selected a scholarly source to go beyond the news. It changed my view that "appeals fix the problem". Appeals rely on people having the time and strength to use them, so the less well-off may end up bearing the harm. That connects to Rawls's difference principle. It also connects to ACS clause 1.2.1, because protecting the public interest means preventing harm, not only fixing it for individuals.

 <img width="1753" height="493" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/a640f243-8ef0-4150-94f9-e39b89c0f59a" />
Figure 4: Allens article on the new ADM transparency rules (Rountree et al. 2026).

 <img width="1116" height="492" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1de4c426-b2a4-4ea5-a439-27ee12349c4e" />


[New Privacy Act rules on ADM start 10 December 2026](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/06/automated-decision-making-transparency-what-app-entities-need-to-know-about-the-app-1-amendments/)
### Summary of the artefact
This article was published by Allens on 10 June 2026 and outlines the changes in the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024. Organisations covered by the Australian Privacy Principles will need to explain in their privacy policies what kinds of personal information they use, and what kinds of decisions are made by, or substantially with, computer programs. It is a transparency rule only. It does not give people any right to challenge a decision (Rountree et al. 2026).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I picked this because it is current and shows the law catching up with ADM. I am a cybersecurity student, and I might be the one who has to document these systems. It relates to ACS clause 1.2.1(g) on privacy (Australian Computer Society 2014, p. 6). It also raises a Kantian question: is it enough to tell people if they have no right to challenge?

Figure 5: RSM Discovery article "Shifting the burden of proof back to AI" (Rotterdam School of Management 2025).

[RSM Discovery article](https://rsm.nl/discovery/2025/shifting-the-burden-of-proof-back-to-ai/)
### Summary of the artefact
This article was published on 14 January 2025 by the Rotterdam School of Management at Erasmus University. It uses the Dutch childcare benefits scandal as its main case. Fraud-detection algorithms over-targeted vulnerable people, and the systems were opaque, which made it extremely difficult to dispute the label. The article says this puts the burden of proof on citizens. It suggests six areas of action, such as public AI registers, human oversight and regular bias testing (Rotterdam School of Management 2025).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I selected the Netherlands as my overseas example because the pattern seemed familiar from Robodebt: the automated system accused people, and they had to prove their innocence. Its suggestion of bias testing is the same as my decision in Question 1. It is also a clear case of using people as a means, which Kantianism opposes, and of a failure of the ACS public interest value.

## Artefact 6:Amnesty International on Denmark's automated welfare state

<img width="1177" height="816" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/678c3c92-78fe-41a3-a973-57935731ddc3" />

Figure 6: Amnesty International press release on Denmark's welfare fraud algorithms (Amnesty International 2024).

[Denmark's welfare fraud algorithms](https://www.amnestyusa.org/press-releases/denmark-eu-ai-powered-welfare-system-fuels-mass-surveillance-and-risks-discriminating-against-marginalized-groups/)

### Summary of the artefact
This Amnesty International press release from 13 November 2024 summarises its report on Denmark's welfare system. Udbetaling Danmark and the company ATP use up to 60 algorithmic models to flag people for fraud investigation. They rely on data, such as citizenship and family relationships, that can act as proxies for race or ethnicity. Amnesty says the system works like social scoring under the EU AI Act (Amnesty International 2024).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I picked a second overseas case to show that this is not only a Dutch problem. The most affected are people with disabilities, people on low incomes and migrants, and the ACS Code says to protect them (Australian Computer Society 2014, pp. 6, 8). It also builds on my e-portfolio 2 artefact on the EU AI Act. It is from 2024, so it is older than my other artefacts, but it is detailed and credible.

## Conclusion 
The same theme comes up in all six of these artefacts: ADM is not neutral, and it is the least powerful who suffer most. Australia has good guidance (Artefacts 2 and 4) but still has failures (Artefact 1), and Carney shows that fixing individual cases is not enough (Artefact 3). The Netherlands and Denmark show the same risks overseas (Artefacts 5 and 6). As an ICT professional, I will ask who is affected, check that the system follows the current law, test it for bias before launch and keep a human involved in decisions that affect people's lives.
