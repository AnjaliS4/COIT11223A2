### Summary of the artefact
This video covers the four types of intellectual property protection: patents for novel inventions (life of 20 years); trademarks for brand identifiers (renewable for 10 years); for know-how (no explanation). It positions each as a distinct balancing act between rewarding a single creator and, over time, returning the creation to public (PatentPC 2026). 

This relates directly to the first slide of our workshop, which discussed how granting time-limited rights to creators encourages creativity, yet it is the public domain that brings the greatest benefit to society once innovations enter it. Prior to the workshop, I believed IP protection existed only to reward creators. That’s changes when we discussed the compromise in slide3: protection is not a reward but a compromise between individual incentive and public benefit (Venugopal 2026). 

### Justification on why I choose the artefact 
I selected this video because, as an ICT professional, I will have to decide what type of protection to use for a product: either public, time-limited protection, or a trade secret such as the Coca-Cola formula (PatentPC 2026). In practice, if I a developing software in my job, deciding whether to make an algorithm open source or treat it as confidential IP is not only a technical and legal choice but also an ethical one: whether it should benefit the public or be more profitable for the company.         

## Artefact 2: Anthropic Copyright Ruling
[Anthropic copyright infringement ruling](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/27/nx-s1-5904606/anthropic-vs-bartz-ai-copyright-lawsuit-pros-cons)

<img width="399" height="501" alt="images" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e89b2c75-c665-487f-9bb7-74a001b40742" />

### Summary of the artefact
AI Company Anthropic settled a class action lawsuit brought by more than 300,000 authors for US $1.5 billion after being ordered to pay for using pirated books to train its Claude models. The ruling was a partial victory for authors: training with legally obtained books is fir use, but training with pirated books is not. The judge left another issue, whether an AI can compete with human writers, untouched  (Veltman 2026). 
The rule utilitarian analysis used in our workshop (Venugopal 2026, slide 42) was applied here to reflect on and justify a live case. The argument from the workshop was: of software is freely copied, the developer does not make much money, less software is created, and society suffers. The same principle applies here: fewer new books are written if authors do not need to be paid for the use of their work, which is bad for society. 

### Justification on why I choose the artefact 
Ethically, the question here isn’t so much whether the copying was authorized as whether it is fair to those whose work enabled the model to write. This is an example of law and ethics not aligning, as our workshop discussed: the court ruled on a narrow legal issue, whether the books were acquired legally, but did not address the ethical issue of whether creators were fairly compensated when their work was used to train a commercial system (Venugopal 2026). As a future ICT professional, this taught me to ask not just whether something is legal, but whether it is fair to the people whose work make it possible as an ICT professional, I should ask both questions, not just the first, when using any system trained on other people’s data. 

## Artefact 3: The Algorithmic Muse and the Public Domain
[Why copyright’s legal philosophy precludes protection for generative AI outputs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clsr.2025.106170)

### Summary of the artefact
Courts have repeatedly been unwilling to recognize non-humans as authors and Elmahjub(2025, p.2) suggests that the same preceded from Naruto vs. Slater, which we discussed in our workshop, applies to AI as well. The article argues that AI- generated content is not necessarily creative, but it still falls into the public domain because it is generated through probability, not human expression, and copyright belongs only to the original creator’s own work, specifically their creative edits to AI-generated content (Elmahjub 2025, p.8). 

### Justification on why I choose the artefact 
This article made me realise that the money selfie case is not an isolated, quirky case, it is a principle that courts have been applying to AI recently: no legal person, no copyright (Venugopal 2026). It reinforced the stance I presented in the workshop discussion: ownership should be tied to the extent of creative decision-making that was truly the human’s own, not simply the human who set the output in motion. 
The reason I chose this artefact is because I think this has ethical considerations for accountability as well. That is, if AI is used to produce harmful content involving a real person, the culpability should lie with the human who directed this, not the AI, because it cannot be held legally or morally responsible. For my future career in ICT, this shapes how I approach credit for AI-assisted creations: I would clearly credit the human part of the creation, while acknowledging that AI involvement is not an excuse to avoid responsibility for how the work is used.  

## Artefact 4: Workshop Reflection- Who owns an AI-generated Image?
**Workshop:** Week 7 | Thursday, 3 September | Campus: Sydney

### Summary of the artefact
In our workshop, we first gave a brief overview of the history of copyright creep in the USA, before looking at the monkey selfie dispute: the camera was set up by photographer David Slater, but the macaque pressed the shutter, and courts rules that animals cannot hold copyright because the law is written for humans (Venugopal 2026). The tutor then extended this to generative AI: if it isn’t owned by a human, who owns an AI-generated image, no one the prompter, or the AI developer? 

### Justification on why I choose the artefact 
Prior to this workshop, I never realized that “creation” equated to ownership. I thought authorship was about who was behind the production, either the photographer who owned the camera or the user who typed the prompt in the case of AI. 

The monkey selfie case changed that. The court did not inquire into who made the photo; it questioned whether the creator was human, and concluded that copyright is a legal construct based on human authorship, not reward for effort or cause (Venugopal 2026). This rethinking directly answered our tutor’s question: if an animal cannot hold copyright, then neither can an AI, because it isn’t human (Elmahjub 2025, p.2). The other thing that changed my mind during the discussion was that ownership should be proportional to the real creative input, rather than automatically assumed by whoever triggered the output. For instance, where the AI user is merely a tool and the person makes the actual creative decisions, they should own the rights to the AI-assisted work. However, if a person instructs an AI to create something unethical, like a harmful image of a real person, the same principle applies: the human is responsible for the harm not the AI. 

This discussion has impacted my future career in ICT by changing how I approach using AI in my work. I now consider this every time I use AI in an assignment, a personal project, or content I post online: how much of the decision-making was truly my own, and would I feel comfortable claiming ownership of it, or being held accountable for it, on that basis. This is the everyday ethical awareness this unit is challenging me to build, and it is something I now actively practice rather than take for granted.
