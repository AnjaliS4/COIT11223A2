### Summary of the artefact
AI Company Anthropic settled a class action lawsuit brought by more than 300,000 authors for US $1.5 billion after being ordered to pay for using pirated books to train its Claude models. The ruling was a partial victory for authors: training with legally obtained books is fir use, but training with pirated books is not. The judge left another issue, whether an AI can compete with human writers, untouched. 
The rule utilitarian analysis used in our workshop was applied here to reflect on and justify a live case. The argument from the workshop was: of software is freely copied, the developer does not make much money, less software is created, and society suffers. The same principle applies here: fewer new books are written if authors do not need to be paid for the use of their work, which is bad for society.

### Justification on why I choose the artefact 
Ethically, the question here isn’t so much whether the copying was authorized as whether it is fair to those whose work enabled the model to write. This is an example of law and ethics not aligning, as our workshop discussed: the court ruled on a narrow legal issue, whether the books were acquired legally, but did not address the ethical issue of whether creators were fairly compensated when their work was used to train a commercial system. As a future ICT professional, this taught me to ask not just whether something is legal, but whether it is fair to the people whose work make it possible as an ICT professional, I should ask both questions, not just the first, when using any system trained on other people’s data.

### Summary of the artefact
Courts have repeatedly been unwilling to recognize non-humans as authors and Elmahjub(2025, p.2) suggests that the same preceded from Naruto vs. Slater, which we discussed in our workshop, applies to AI as well. The article argues that AI- generated content is not necessarily creative, but it still falls into the public domain because it is generated through probability, not human expression, and copyright belongs only to the original creator’s own work, specifically their creative edits to AI-generated content (Elmahjub 2025, p.8). 
The monkey selfie case made me see that this is not an isolated, quirky case, it is a principle that courts have been applying to AI recently: no legal person, no copyright. It reinforced the stance I presented in the workshop discussion: ownership should be tied to the extent of creative decision-making that was truly the human’s own, not simply the human who set the output in motion. 

### Justification on why I choose the artefact 
The reason I chose this artefact is because I think this has ethical considerations for accountability as well. That is, if AI is used to produce harmful content involving a real person, the culpability should lie with the human who directed this, not the AI, because it cannot be held legally or morally responsible. For my future career in ICT, this shapes how I approach credit for AI-assisted creations: I would clearly credit the human part of the creation, while acknowledging that AI involvement is not an excuse to avoid responsibility for how the work is used.
