## Artefact 1: eSafety Commissioner vs X (2024)

[eSafety drops case against Elon Musk's X over church stabbing videos](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-06-05/esafety-elon-musk-x-church-stabbing-videos-court-case/103937152)

<img width="864" height="612" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e78db9af-3eee-4302-941e-439cf1b38f44" />


### Summary of the artefact
Australia's eSafety Commissioner demanded that X (formerly Twitter) delete all videos of the Wakeley church stabbing worldwide, under the Online Safety Act. 
The Federal Court ruled that X was only required to geo-block the content within Australia, not delete it globally. As reported by Evans and Butler (2024), the Commissioner responded by asking,
"Why is it OK to have that content on the internet?", illustrating the limits of national law over international platforms.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
From an ethical viewpoint, this case reflects Mill's Harm Principle: the stabbing video caused real harm by inciting further violence, which is exactly the kind of harm Mill says justifies restricting expression, not just disliking it (Quinn 2020). 
Previously, I believed the word "censorship" was inherently the wrong side of any debate; this case has changed that. I now believe the issue isn't really whether to censor, but whether the censorship is proportionate to a real, proven harm rather than just discomfort. 
Moving forward, specifically in my cyber security journey, whenever I am asked to help implement or contest a content policy at my workplace, I want to use that same harm-based test, rather than my gut, when deciding whether to allow or block something.

## Artefact 2: Nepal's Social Media Ban (2025)

[Nepal lifts social media ban after 19 killed in protests against corruption](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/9/9/nepal-lifts-social-media-ban-after-19-killed-in-protests-report)

<img width="965" height="534" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/50502bf9-7d0f-4025-9b40-32fe09a9d401" />



### Summary of the artefact
Nepal's government blocked 26 social media platforms, including Facebook, WhatsApp and YouTube, after the companies missed a government deadline to register. 
Within days, youth-led "Gen Z" protests against corruption and the ban escalated into deadly violence, resulting in 19 deaths and more than 300 injuries. 
The government lifted the ban within 24 hours of the worst violence, and the Prime Minister resigned shortly afterwards.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
Ethically, Nepal's ban fails the test set by Mill, since it restricted an entire population's expression to correct a narrow registration and tax issue, with no real harm threshold behind it. 
This is the very "backward step" Kant warns against when he argues that barriers to people using their own reason should be removed, not added (Quinn 2020). This is personal for me, since it's my home country, and it changed how I read government "protecting the public" language. 
I used to take safety justifications for internet restrictions fairly literally, even in Australia's case, but seeing my own country's government use that language to mask a registration and revenue problem means I now read every future "protecting the public" claim far more critically.

## Artefact 3: Australia's Under-16 Social Media Ban (2025–2026)

[High Court agrees to hear teenagers' challenge to under 16s social media ban](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-04/court-agrees-hear-teens-challenge-to-under-16-social-media-ban/106103338)

<img width="936" height="811" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/03cff52d-af46-4f0a-8fd8-6b673494e8fc" />



### Summary of the artefact
On 10 December 2025, Australia became the first country in the world to ban social media accounts for under-16s. The law was challenged in the High Court by two 15-year-olds, backed by the Digital Freedom Project, who argued it breaches the "implied freedom of political communication". 
The Court agreed to hear the case, but it remains undecided, a live constitutional question about how far governments can go to protect children online.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
Ethically, this is where Kant and Mill point in different directions, and I chose it because that tension remains unresolved for me. 
Kant would likely see an age-based ban as depriving a group of the chance to use their own reason before they've had the opportunity to develop it, whereas Mill's Harm Principle could still support the ban if the harm to children is severe and proven (Quinn 2020). 
Before researching this, I thought protecting children from social media was ethically straightforward; now I see it as a genuine dilemma between two defensible principled positions, rather than a settled legal question. 
From now on, I want to apply that same "which principle actually fits this harm" test before assuming that any protective policy, for kids, workers, or users in general, is automatically the ethical one.

**Workshop:** Week 9 | Thursday, 17 September | Campus: Sydney

### Summary of the artefact
In Week 9's workshop we looked at the Daily Stormer case: the neo-Nazi site claimed that Cloudflare's continued service meant Cloudflare supported their ideology, and in response, Cloudflare dropped the site as a customer, despite its CEO admitting this made it "a little bit harder... to argue against a government somewhere pressuring us into taking down a site". 
It was the first time Cloudflare had ever removed a customer because of political pressure.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
neutrality gave Daily Stormer a platform for years, and it wasn't the hate itself that caused the drop, it was being publicly framed as endorsing it, which is closer to reputation management than to Mill's harm-based justification for restricting expression (Quinn 2020). 
I used to believe that a company staying neutral on political and ideological viewpoints was automatically the more ethical choice; this case showed me that neutrality can itself enable harm if it's applied without ever asking whether real damage is being done. 
Moving forward, particularly in tech, I'll judge platform decisions by whether they're actually responding to real harm, not by whether "neutral" or "decisive" sounds better in the news.
