## Artefact 1: EU Data Act and EU AI Act Explained

[The EU Data Act and the Artificial Intelligence (AI) Act: new laws on data](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u37w2519HK0)

### Summary of the artefact

This interview breaks down the EU AI Act's risk-based structure: minimal-risk systems like spam filters face no real obligations, "limited risk" tools like chatbots need transparency disclosures, "higher risk" sectors like healthcare and law enforcement face stricter rules, and "unacceptable risk" practices are banned outright. Non-compliance penalties can reach €35 million or 7% of global turnover, and the Act applies extraterritorially to any non-EU business whose AI systems are used within the EU.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

I picked this because it fills the gap Artefact 2 left open — the EU announcement tells us new rules exist, but this explains what actually enforces them: real, tiered risk categories with real fines. It's the "clearly named accountable authority" Chung and Schiff (2025, p. 622) call for, made concrete — though the closing question about non-EU businesses shows the sovereign's reach still has real limits.

## Artefact 2: Safer and More Transparent AI — EU AI Act Transparency Rules

[Safer and more transparent AI](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/safer-and-more-transparent-ai-2026-08-02_en)

### Summary of the artefact

New EU AI Act transparency rules became legally enforceable on 2 August 2026, requiring AI systems to disclose when someone is interacting with AI or viewing AI-generated content, with real penalties for non-compliance. The European Commission frames this as protecting citizens from deception and manipulation as AI becomes more embedded in daily life.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

This is Hobbes' social contract playing out in real time: a governing authority imposing binding rules on companies in exchange for collective safety. Chung and Schiff (2025, p. 622) argue AI governance needs "a legally accountable authority clearly named" — which is exactly what the EU Act provides. It made me question whether this counts as genuine submission to protection, or just enforcement dressed up as consent.

## Artefact 3: AI and the Social Contract

[AI and the Social Contract](https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v8i1.36575)

### Summary of the artefact

Chung and Schiff (2025, p. 615) evaluate AI governance against five social contract theories — Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls, and Nozick — finding legitimacy and consent deficits in all of them. They note Hobbes' state-of-nature "agreement" was always driven by fear rather than genuine consent, which blurs the line between people reasonably agreeing to be governed and simply being forced to submit (p. 621).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

I chose this because it directly answers what our workshop left open: does the state of nature actually justify submission, or just excuse it? Chung and Schiff (2025, p. 622) suggest one fix is requiring "a legally accountable authority clearly named" for every AI system — which is precisely the enforcement mechanism Artefacts 1 and 2 both show being built in practice.

## Artefact 4: Workshop Reflection – Submission, Rules, and Who's Above Them

**Workshop:** Week 4 | Thursday, 6 August | Campus: Sydney

![Workshop Selfie](images/workshop-selfie-week4.jpg)

### Summary of the artefact

In Week 4's workshop we covered social contract theory: Hobbes' claim that life in the state of nature is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," and that we implicitly submit to a sovereign — accepting leaders, workers, and soldiers — because it beats being in the wild. Rousseau added a condition Hobbes never gave: legitimacy holds only if "no one is above the rules."

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

This stuck with me because it's not really about ancient philosophy — it's happening with AI right now. The same objection our slides raised against Hobbes, that no one actually signed a contract, is the exact gap researchers found in AI governance too (Chung & Schiff 2025). Makes me wonder if we're repeating history's oldest excuse for submission, just with a new sovereign.
